The Salt in Your Sukuma: Eating to Lower Blood Pressure the Kenyan Way

By Sarran Mahonye Otieno · October 2026 · 2 min read

High blood pressure (hypertension) is often called a silent killer because most people feel completely fine until it causes a stroke, heart attack or kidney damage. Many young Kenyans have never had their blood pressure checked. If you haven’t in the last year, make that your first step: it takes two minutes at most pharmacies.

Where the salt really comes from

The World Health Organization recommends less than 5 g of salt a day, about one level teaspoon in total, from all sources. Most of us eat far more, and it’s rarely only from the salt shaker:

  • Stock cubes and seasoning powders: one cube can contain over half a day’s salt.
  • Processed meats: sausages, smokies, bacon and ham.
  • Bread, crisps and salted snacks: small amounts add up over a day.
  • Soups, sauces and instant noodles.
  • Extra salt at the table on food that was already salted in cooking.

The DASH approach, Kenyan style

DASH stands for Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension, an eating pattern shown in clinical trials to lower blood pressure. The principles translate easily to local food:

  • Lots of vegetables and fruit, rich in potassium: sukuma, managu, spinach, tomatoes, bananas, avocado, oranges and pawpaw.
  • Whole grains: unsifted maize flour, brown rice, millet and sorghum.
  • Legumes, nuts and seeds: beans, ndengu, njahi, groundnuts.
  • Low-fat dairy: milk, mala and yoghurt.
  • Fish and chicken more often than red and processed meat.

Cooking with less salt without losing flavour

  1. Use onions, garlic, ginger, dhania, lemon and tomatoes as your flavour base.
  2. Try dried herbs and spices such as cumin, pilau masala, black pepper, turmeric and chilli.
  3. Cut stock cubes in half, then try a week without them. Taste buds adjust in about two to three weeks.
  4. Taste before you salt at the table.
  5. Read labels: compare sodium per 100 g and choose the lower option.

It’s not only about salt

Keeping a healthy weight, staying active, limiting alcohol, not smoking and managing stress all lower blood pressure. If you already take blood pressure medicine, keep taking it as prescribed: food changes work alongside your medication, not instead of it. Some potassium-rich diets need adjusting for people with kidney disease, which is why personalised advice matters.

The takeaway

Know your numbers, cut back on stock cubes and processed meat, and load your plate with potassium-rich greens and fruit. Small daily changes add up to real protection for your heart.

This article is general education, not personal medical advice. If you have a medical condition, are pregnant, or take medication, speak to your doctor or book a consultation before changing your diet.
