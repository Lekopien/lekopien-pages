Ugali, Chapati and Blood Sugar: A Kenyan Plate for Type 2 Diabetes

By Sarran Mahonye Otieno · October 2026 · 2 min read

One of the first things many of my clients with type 2 diabetes say is: “The doctor told me to stop eating ugali.” Then they try, feel miserable, and give up. Here’s the truth: you can keep eating the foods you grew up with. What matters most is how much, what you eat with it and how often.

Why carbohydrates matter in diabetes

All carbohydrates (ugali, rice, chapati, bread, potatoes, fruit, sugar) are broken down into glucose. In type 2 diabetes the body doesn’t respond to insulin as well, so glucose stays higher in the blood. Large portions of refined carbs eaten on their own cause the biggest spikes. Fibre, protein and fat slow that rise down.

The Kenyan diabetes plate

Use a standard dinner plate, about 23 cm across:

  • Half the plate: non-starchy vegetables such as sukuma, managu, cabbage, spinach, terere, tomatoes, carrots and kachumbari.
  • A quarter: protein such as fish, chicken, lean beef, eggs, beans, ndengu or lentils.
  • A quarter: starch. A piece of ugali about the size of your fist, one small chapati, or a fist of rice or sweet potato.

Add water or unsweetened tea, and a piece of fruit later rather than a soda with the meal.

Swaps that make a real difference

Instead ofTry
Sifted white maize flourWhole-maize flour (unsifted), or ugali mixed with millet or sorghum
White chapatiChapati made with half whole-wheat flour, and one instead of three
Tea with two spoons of sugarTea without sugar, or gradually cut down by half a spoon a week
Juice or sodaWater, or whole fruit (the fibre slows the sugar)
Mandazi for breakfastEggs, arrowroot or sweet potato, or uji made from whole grains without added sugar

Meal timing

Eating regular meals, rather than skipping breakfast and having a huge dinner, keeps blood sugar more stable. If you take insulin or medicines like sulphonylureas, skipping meals can cause low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia), so always discuss changes in your eating pattern with your doctor or dietitian.

Movement helps your food work better

A 10 to 15 minute walk after meals helps your muscles take up glucose. It’s one of the simplest, most effective habits for people with type 2 diabetes.

The takeaway

Keep the ugali, shrink the portion, double the greens, add protein, and walk after you eat. Then check your numbers: your glucose readings and HbA1c will tell you how well your plate is working for you.

This article is general education, not personal medical advice. If you have a medical condition, are pregnant, or take medication, speak to your doctor or book a consultation before changing your diet.
