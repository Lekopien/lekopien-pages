The Oontz Head’s Guide to Rave Recovery: A Dietitian’s Guide to Electrolytes & Inflammation

By Sarran Mahonye Otieno · October 2026 · 2 min read

Yes, I’m a dietitian. Yes, I’ll be at the front left by the speakers. This guide isn’t about telling you not to go out. It’s about understanding what a long night of dancing does to your body, so you can enjoy it and still make Monday’s gym session.

What six hours of dancing actually does

  • You sweat a lot. Dancing in a hot, crowded room can cost well over a litre of sweat across a night. Sweat carries sodium, plus some potassium.
  • Alcohol makes it worse. It suppresses the hormone that helps you hold onto water, so you pee more and dehydrate faster.
  • Sleep loss raises stress hormones. Cortisol stays high, appetite hormones go haywire, and you crave salty, fatty food the next day.
  • Your muscles are tired. Hours on your feet create the same small-scale muscle damage and inflammation as a long workout.

Before you go

  1. Eat a real meal 2 to 3 hours before: carbs, protein and some salt. Think rice, beans and chicken, or chapati and ndengu.
  2. Arrive hydrated, not overloaded. A couple of glasses of water in the evening is enough.

During the night

  • Sip, don’t chug. Roughly a cup of water every hour while you’re dancing hard is a sensible starting point. Drinking huge amounts of plain water quickly can dilute your blood sodium to dangerous levels (hyponatraemia), especially if you are also using stimulants.
  • Alternate each alcoholic drink with a glass of water.
  • Take breaks in a cooler area. Overheating is a bigger risk than most people think.
If you or a friend becomes confused, stops sweating while very hot, has a severe headache, vomits repeatedly or collapses, that is a medical emergency. Get help from venue medics or go to the nearest hospital straight away.

The day-after checklist

  1. Rehydrate with electrolytes. Oral rehydration salts (ORS) from any pharmacy are cheap and formulated to be absorbed well. Mix them exactly as the packet says. Coconut water or a pinch of salt with some fruit juice in water also helps.
  2. Eat breakfast with protein and carbs. Eggs with toast or sweet potato, or uji with milk and groundnuts. Protein supplies the amino acids your body uses for repair and for making brain chemicals; carbs refill your glycogen.
  3. Add colour. Fruit and vegetables (oranges, pawpaw, tomatoes, sukuma, beetroot) provide antioxidants that support recovery.
  4. Use ginger and turmeric in food. Ginger tea can settle a queasy stomach, and turmeric in your stew is a tasty way to add anti-inflammatory compounds. They support recovery but they are not magic cures.
  5. Move gently. A walk or light stretching beats a heavy leg day.
  6. Protect your sleep. A short nap before 3pm, then an early night. Sleep is the most powerful recovery tool you have.

The takeaway

Eat before you go, sip water steadily, respect the heat, and use ORS and a proper meal the next day. Your body will forgive you much faster, and you’ll be back on the dancefloor next weekend.

This article is general education, not personal medical advice. If you have a medical condition, are pregnant, or take medication, speak to your doctor or book a consultation before changing your diet.
