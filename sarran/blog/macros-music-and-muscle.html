Macros, Music, and Muscle: Why Restrictive Dieting is Ruining Your Energy

By Sarran Mahonye Otieno · October 2026 · 2 min read

Every few months a new “cut” sweeps through Nairobi’s gyms. One meal a day. Juice only until 4pm. 800 calories and two hours of cardio. The before-and-after photos look great on Instagram. What they don’t show is the person who can’t finish a set, snaps at their colleagues by 3pm, and is asleep before the DJ has even started.

Food is fuel, not the enemy. When you starve the engine, it doesn’t run cleaner. It runs slower.

What happens when you eat too little

Your body needs a baseline amount of energy just to keep your heart beating, your brain thinking and your hormones working. Sports scientists call what’s left after training energy availability. When it drops too low for too long, the body starts protecting itself:

  • Your metabolism adapts. You burn less energy at rest as the body downshifts non-essential work. This is one reason crash dieters often stall, then regain weight quickly.
  • Hormones go quiet. Thyroid activity, testosterone and oestrogen can all fall. In women this can mean irregular or missing periods, which is a warning sign, not a badge of honour.
  • You lose muscle, not just fat. Without enough protein and energy, the body breaks down muscle for fuel, which is the exact opposite of what most gym-goers want.
  • Your bones and immune system suffer. Long-term low energy availability is linked with stress fractures and getting sick more often.

This cluster of effects now has a name: Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S). It doesn’t only happen to elite athletes. It shows up in regular people who train hard and eat like they’re trying to disappear.

Why you feel so flat

Carbohydrate is your muscles’ preferred fuel for high-intensity work. Cut it to near zero and your glycogen stores (the carbs stored in muscle and liver) run low. Heavy lifts feel heavier, sprints feel slower, and the brain, which runs mostly on glucose, starts to complain with headaches, poor focus and irritability. Add poor sleep and caffeine on an empty stomach, and you have the classic Nairobi burnout cocktail.

A better way to lose fat

  1. Make the deficit small. A modest deficit, roughly 300 to 500 calories below maintenance, lets most people lose fat steadily while still training well.
  2. Keep protein high. Aim for protein at every meal: eggs, beans, ndengu, omena, chicken, beef, milk or mala. It protects muscle and keeps you full.
  3. Time your carbs. Put more of your ugali, rice, sweet potato or bananas around your training sessions, and less late at night after a rest day.
  4. Eat enough vegetables to fill half the plate. Sukuma, managu, terere, cabbage and tomatoes add volume and fibre for very few calories.
  5. Plan for the weekend. If you know you’ll be out dancing for six hours on Saturday, eat a proper meal before you go. Your Sunday self will thank you.

Red flags to watch for

If you are always cold, always tired, losing your period, getting injured often, or thinking about food all day, your diet is costing you more than it’s giving. That’s exactly the moment to talk to a registered dietitian rather than trying a stricter plan.
