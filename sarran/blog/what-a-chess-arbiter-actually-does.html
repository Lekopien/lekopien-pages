The Arbiter’s Corner

What a Chess Arbiter Actually Does (and Why You Should Care)

By Sarran Mahonye Otieno · October 2026 · 2 min read

People are often surprised to hear that chess has officials. “What is there to referee? Nobody tackles anybody.” But anyone who has played a tense last-round game with a few seconds on the clock knows exactly why arbiters exist.

The arbiter’s job in one sentence

An arbiter makes sure the FIDE Laws of Chess and the tournament regulations are followed, so that every player gets a fair game in a calm playing hall. That covers pairings, starting the rounds, watching games, handling disputes and claims, keeping order and submitting results for rating.

When we step in, and when we don’t

A big part of being a good arbiter is knowing when not to intervene. We never point out that a player is about to blunder, and we don’t tell a player that their opponent forgot to press the clock. Many claims, such as a draw by repetition, are up to the players to make. We do step in when:

  • an illegal move has been completed,
  • a flag has fallen and we observe it (under current FIDE Laws the arbiter calls it),
  • a player is disturbing their opponent or others,
  • there is any suspicion of cheating or outside help,
  • a player makes a claim, such as a draw by threefold repetition.

Five rules every tournament player should know

  1. Touch-move. If you deliberately touch one of your pieces, you must move it if it has a legal move. If you touch an opponent’s piece, you must capture it if you can. To straighten a piece, say “I adjust” before touching it.
  2. Same hand. Press the clock with the same hand you used to make your move.
  3. Illegal moves cost you. In standard games, the first completed illegal move usually gives your opponent extra time; a second one in the same game loses it. Rapid and blitz follow similar rules, so don’t assume you’ll get a free warning.
  4. Offering a draw. Make your move, offer the draw, then press your clock. Don’t keep offering; repeatedly doing so can be treated as disturbing your opponent.
  5. Phones and devices. Most events don’t allow a phone that is switched on in the playing area. If it rings, you can lose the game. Read the event regulations and leave it in your bag, switched off, or with the arbiter.

Why you should care

Knowing the rules is a competitive advantage. Players who understand claims, clocks and touch-move stay calm in chaotic positions and don’t give away points through technicalities. And if something feels wrong in your game, stop the clocks and call the arbiter. That’s what we’re there for.

Rules referenced here follow the FIDE Laws of Chess. Event regulations can change some details (for example, mobile phone rules or time controls), so always read the regulations of the tournament you are playing.
