The Arbiter’s Corner

Lessons from the Arbiter’s Table: Mistakes I See Players Make

By Sarran Mahonye Otieno · October 2026 · 2 min read

Arbiters see hundreds of games. The same mistakes come up again and again, and almost none of them are about chess skill. Here are the ones that cost players the most.

1. Losing on time in a winning position

This is the most painful one to watch. A player builds a beautiful position, then spends 25 minutes on one move and has to play the last ten moves in a panic. The fix: check your clock every few moves, and set yourself time checkpoints, for example “by move 20 I should still have half my time”. A good move played in time beats the perfect move played too late.

2. Not knowing what to do after an illegal move

If your opponent makes an illegal move, don’t just correct it yourself and carry on, and don’t sweep pieces off the board in frustration. Stop the clocks and call the arbiter. The position is restored and the right penalty is applied.

3. Touching pieces while thinking

Hovering, tapping or “testing” a piece by touching it is a habit from casual games. In a tournament, touch-move applies. Keep your hands off the board until you have decided.

4. Phones and smart devices

Every season someone loses a game because a phone rang or buzzed in their pocket. It’s heartbreaking, and completely avoidable. Switch it off and put it away, or leave it outside the playing area as the regulations require.

5. Arguing with the opponent instead of calling the arbiter

Disputes escalate quickly at the board. Pause the clock, raise your hand and let the arbiter handle it. It keeps the game fair and keeps you focused.

6. Messy scoresheets

A scoresheet that’s missing moves makes it very hard to prove a threefold repetition or the 50-move rule. Write clearly, every move, until the rules allow you to stop in time trouble.

7. Bad sportsmanship

Not shaking hands, resigning by knocking pieces over, or celebrating loudly while others are still playing all disturb the hall. Chess is a small community, and people remember how you behave.

The takeaway

Most lost points at amateur level are self-inflicted. Manage your clock, know the basic procedures, and keep calm. You’ll pick up points without improving a single opening line.

Rules referenced here follow the FIDE Laws of Chess. Event regulations can change some details (for example, mobile phone rules or time controls), so always read the regulations of the tournament you are playing.
